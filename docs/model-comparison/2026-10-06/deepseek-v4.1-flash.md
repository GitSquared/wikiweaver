# The Thirty-Day Road Census

The **Thirty-Day Road Census** is the recurring survey of living roads maintained by the signatory settlements of the [[Lantern Compact]]. It is conducted only during the [[Thirty-Day Eclipse]], when silver moss becomes dormant and the geometry of grown roads is temporarily fixed. The census records road intervals rather than exact lengths, gathers boundary evidence, and produces woven moss maps that are deposited at the open-air [[Reed Archive]] in [[Halewick]]. Because road moss cannot grow underground, no part of the census archive is kept in vaults or subterranean chambers.

## Name and legal basis

The name *Thirty-Day Road Census* refers both to the survey season and to the body of records produced during it. The census is not a single event but a recurring constitutional practice. Under the [[Lantern Compact]], ratified in 214 of the [[Quiet Calendar]] by the seven coastal settlements, road surveys must be performed during the thirty-day eclipse. The compact also forbids boundary revisions until all seven settlements have countersigned the survey. The first Thirty-Day Road Census took place in 219 QC. Later censuses were convened at irregular intervals until the [[Tidewatch Concord]] of 241 fixed a formal cycle of one full census per eclipse occurrence.

## Survey season

Census work begins at the first dimming of the thirty-day eclipse. During this period, silver moss is dormant and does not extend, contract, or alter its root-beds. Surveyors therefore treat the roads as temporarily stable, though never as exact. All work must be completed before moonlight returns and growth resumes. Parties travel on foot and by [[wind sled]]; no engines or electronics are used. A typical party includes a [[Census Warden]], two or more boundary clerks, a knot-reader, and a map-weaver from the [[Moss Weavers' Assembly]]. Supplies are carried in reed panniers and checked by the [[Bracken Survey Guild]].

## The paired-knot method

The census relies on the [[paired-knot method]] devised by [[Sera Venn]] in 217 QC. A paired knot records an interval of possible road lengths rather than one exact length. At each endpoint of a road segment, surveyors tie two knots at a measured separation; the resulting interval, known as a [[Venn Interval]], can be compared with later censuses even as the living road slowly changes shape. Written measurements are considered approximate and are always accompanied by knot intervals. Although Venn's technique became standard, Venn was a map-weaver and never held the office of [[Chief Surveyor]].

## Archive and custody

Census maps are kept at the open-air [[Reed Archive]] in [[Halewick]]. The archive has no underground vaults because road moss cannot grow underground. Its screens and louvers are adjusted by the [[Order of the Reed]] so that stored map-moss receives moonlight without becoming tangled or overgrown. After each eclipse, archivists trim minor growth and record any drift in the woven boundaries. A census map is not considered authoritative unless it retains its paired knots and bears the countersignatures of all seven settlements. Copies without such marks are treated as working drafts.

## History

The first Thirty-Day Road Census in 219 QC established the basic procedure of knot-taking, countersignature, and open-air storage. Early censuses were often incomplete because some settlements delayed their survey parties or disputed access to shared roads. The disputed census of 263 QC produced two incompatible maps. The settlements of [[Marrow Fen]] and [[Kestrel Verge]] each submitted a boundary map that disagreed over a stretch of moss road near the [[Glass Harbor]] approaches. Rather than destroy either map, the settlements preserved both as the [[Twin Census Maps of 263]] and marked the shared boundary as the [[Provisional Boundary of 263]]. No settlement redrew the boundary unilaterally. This precedent, later called the [[Twin Map Accord]], remains a guiding principle in census disputes.

## Significance

The Thirty-Day Road Census is central to boundary negotiation among the coastal settlements. It substitutes woven evidence for warfare, allowing changes to be debated through intervals, knots, and countersignatures rather than through armed conflict. The census also supports route safety, wind-sled scheduling, and repair priorities. Because living roads slowly change shape, later surveyors must interpret old maps alongside fresh knot readings. The census is thus both a record and a living object: it requires open air, moonlight, and careful tending, and its authority rests as much on preserved uncertainty as on measured distance.
