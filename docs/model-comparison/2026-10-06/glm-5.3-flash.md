# The Thirty-Day Road Census

The **Thirty-Day Road Census** is a recurring cartographic survey conducted across the seven coastal settlements bound by the [[Lantern Compact]]. Held during the thirty-day eclipse—when road moss lies dormant and no living road can grow, shift, or wither—the census establishes a fixed, momentary record of the network of cultivated silver roads connecting the settlements. It is regarded as the most consequential ritual of measurement on the moon, and the only occasion on which boundary negotiations may formally proceed.

## Origins

The census was mandated by the [[Lantern Compact]], ratified in Quiet Calendar 214 by the seven coastal settlements following the [[Siltward Feuds]], a decade of low-grade territorial friction in which rival map-weavers produced contradictory woven charts of the [[Halewick approaches]]. The Compact's framers reasoned that only during the eclipse, when moss is inert and roads hold their shape, could any survey claim temporary authority. The Compact further forbids boundary revisions until all seven settlements have countersigned the survey, a provision intended to prevent any single settlement from unilaterally redrawing a shared edge.

The first census took place in Quiet Calendar 219, five years after ratification, allowing time for the settlement of [[Ondrel's Rest]] and [[Marrowgate]] to train sufficient survey crews and for the completion of the [[Reed Archive]] at Halewick, the open-air repository where all census maps have since been kept. The Archive maintains no underground vaults, since road moss cannot grow underground and woven moss maps require open moonlight to remain supple.

## Method

Because the census must be completed within the span of the eclipse, survey teams travel exclusively on foot and by wind sled, as no engines or electronics exist to hasten the work. Each settlement dispatches a survey party along its assigned stretches of road, knotting measurements into moss-thread field cords as they walk. Measurement is necessarily approximate; even a dormant road resumes its slow reshaping once moonlight returns, and census records are understood to describe the road *as it stood*, never as it will remain.

The dominant recording technique is the [[paired-knot method]], devised by the map-weaver [[Sera Venn]] in Quiet Calendar 217, two years before the first census. Rather than committing to a single length, a paired knot records an interval of possible road lengths—an upper and lower bound between which the road is judged to fall. Venn, who never held the office of chief surveyor, defended the method in her treatise *On Honest Cordage*, arguing that a single knot "flatters the road with a certainty it never offered." The method was formally adopted at the [[Second Mending of Halewick]] in Quiet Calendar 218 and has been used in every census since.

At the conclusion of the eclipse, field cords are returned to the Reed Archive, where map-weavers translate them into woven moss charts within the following lunar season. Countersignature ceremonies traditionally coincide with the [[First Return of Moonlight]], the festival marking the end of dormancy.

## Disputes

The census has twice failed to produce a unanimous chart. The most serious rupture occurred in Quiet Calendar 263, when survey parties from Marrowgate and [[Thessaly Reach]] returned incompatible measurements of their shared boundary, producing two mutually exclusive maps. In accordance with Compact custom, the settlements declined to destroy either chart or redraw the boundary by decree. Instead, both maps were preserved in the Reed Archive, and the contested boundary was formally designated the [[Provisional Edge of 263]], a designation it retains to this day, with settlements crossing it obliged to announce their intent at the boundary cairns.

## Cultural significance

The census is widely regarded as the closest thing the coastal settlements possess to a shared institution. Poets have described it as "the month the roads agree to hold still," and schoolchildren in [[Cinderhollow]] traditionally learn knotwork by reproducing simplified paired-knot cords from the census of their birth year. Critics, chiefly among the inland [[Moorland Weavers]], contend that the census privileges coastal settlements and enshrines a false momentary precision over the living truth of roads; their objection is recorded, respectfully and without resolution, in the marginalia of every Archive chart since 240.
